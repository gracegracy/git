# A. SECTION 2.1.1: DEFINITIONS PARAGRAPH

> *In the paragraph that clarifies the thesis's concepts, replace the two sentences beginning "Similarly, technology capability refers to the ability of an economy or system…" with the text below.*

The thesis also distinguishes technological capability from technology development level. Technological capability is an ability: the ability of an organisation or an economy to create, absorb, adapt and apply technological knowledge, which is built up over time in firms and national economies (Lall, 1992; Yeon et al., 2021). Technology development level is the level of technological development that a country has already achieved, as shown by observable outcomes such as patents, technology exports and value added in technology-intensive industries (Archibugi & Coco, 2005; Desai et al., 2002). The first is a capacity; the second is the result of that capacity. Chapter 4 uses the second concept as a national-level construct, measured in transport-related fields, and examines how it is associated with innovation input, government incentives and participation in technology projects.

**Technology development level of a country**

Technology development level refers to the level of technological development that a country has achieved in a given field. It is related to, but distinct from, technological capability. Technological capability is an ability. Lall (1992) analysed how such capabilities are built up in firms and national economies, and Yeon et al. (2021) distinguish a "concept design capability", the ability to create new blueprints, models or standards, from an "implementation capability", the ability to realise designs and improve manufacturing efficiency. Abilities of this kind are difficult to observe directly at the national level. Studies that compare countries therefore rely on observable indicators, such as patents, scientific output, technology exports and the diffusion of technologies, which are often combined into composite indices (Archibugi & Coco, 2005; Archibugi et al., 2009). The Technology Achievement Index, for example, assesses a country's technological achievements in terms of the creation and diffusion of new technologies, the diffusion of older technologies and the development of human skills (Desai et al., 2002). Such indicators show the level of development that a country has reached rather than the abilities that produced it.

Following this measurement approach, this chapter defines the technology development level of a country as the level of technological development that the country has achieved in transport-related fields, as reflected in its technology outputs. Because the construct is measured by outputs, it captures the realised result of national technological capabilities rather than the capabilities themselves, and the resources that feed the innovation process are measured separately as national innovation input. The construct is defined at the country level because the participation indicators are also aggregated at that level: the project data measure the total involvement of organisations located in each sample country. Previous studies have shown that firm-level technological capabilities and firm characteristics matter for innovation and for the effects of public R&D support (Schoenecker & Swanson, 2002; Wanzenböck et al., 2013), but analyses at the micro level can neglect broader national factors. In the published versions of this chapter (Li & Lam, 2022, 2025), the construct is referred to as technology capability or technological capability. It is renamed in this thesis to reflect more precisely that it is measured by technology outputs.

In this chapter, the technology development level is reflected by three output indicators: 1) the number of patents in triadic patent families in the civil engineering and transport technology domains; 2) medium- and high-technology exports in the transport sector; and 3) value added in the manufacture of transport equipment and in transport and storage.

The number of patents reflects the creation of new technology. Patent counts are a standard output measure in comparative studies of national innovation; Furman et al. (2002), for example, use international patenting as the output of a country's national innovative capacity. Medium- and high-technology exports reflect a country's ability to produce technologically advanced goods that compete in international markets. At the firm level, export behaviour has been found to depend on innovation outputs rather than on innovation inputs (Tavassoli, 2018), which supports the use of exports as an output-side indicator. Value added in the manufacture of transport equipment and in transport and storage reflects the productive outcome of technology in these industries. Manufacturing value added, and the share of medium- and high-technology activities in it, are among the indicators that UNIDO uses to measure countries' industrial capacity and technological upgrading in its Competitive Industrial Performance Index (UNIDO, n.d.).

## B3. Section 4.3.2: hypotheses

> *Replace the paragraphs under "Hypothesis description" from "Based on national innovation system theory, the government plays multiple roles…" to H3 with the text below. The paragraph on innovation input and firm-level outputs (Cruz-Cázares et al., 2013; Parthasarthy & Hammond, 2002) is unchanged except for its last two sentences, which are given here. In the paragraph before H4, change "…government incentives not only directly affect the technology capability to generate innovative outcomes…" to "…government incentives not only directly affect the generation of innovative outcomes, and hence the technology development level,…".*

Based on national innovation system theory, the government plays multiple roles in affecting the technology development level of a country. In this chapter, the technology development level reflects the technological output achieved in transport-related fields, and government incentives for R&D are also measured for transport infrastructure-related industries, as described in the indicator definitions. According to national innovation system theory, government support is important for technological development, as the government can act as a regulator, a sponsor, a user or even a buyer in the process of technology commercialisation (Caerteling et al., 2008). Government support takes multiple forms, including direct funds and indirect incentives, which may have different effects. Guan and Yam (2015) studied firms in Beijing, China, during the 1990s and found that direct government financial incentives failed to increase the patents of either high-tech or general firms, and that explicit earmarks even had a negative effect. However, studies of Australian and Brazilian firms have shown that direct incentives are crucial to improving firms' innovation capabilities (Yigitcanlar et al., 2019), which underpin the technological outputs measured in this chapter.

Generally, encouraging policies, such as lower taxes and favourable compensation through funds and subsidies, can fuel R&D and the generation of new technologies. It is therefore hypothesised that government incentives for R&D are associated with more technology being created and diffused, and hence with a higher technology development level. From the demand-pull view, the relationship may also run in the opposite direction: a higher technology development level, with more innovation outputs, may influence policymakers' decisions on how much effort to devote to encouraging R&D. The reverse relationship is therefore also tested in this chapter. The first hypothesis is as follows:

**H1: Government incentives for R&D are positively related to the technology development level of a country.**

> *Second hypothesis paragraph (on demand-pull and technology-push, Cruz-Cázares et al., 2013): replace the sentence citing Sultana and Turkina (2020) with the first sentence below. Its current wording ("technology absorptive ability … could positively affect technological superiority") goes beyond what the study reports. Then replace the last two sentences before H2 with the second and third sentences below.*

In studies of technology transfer and foreign direct investment, a country's absorptive capacity, indicated by R&D, human capital and knowledge intensity, has been found to strengthen the association between its position in international investment networks and its technology status (Sultana & Turkina, 2020).

In this chapter, the technology development level of a country reflects the technology production outcomes that the country has achieved. It is therefore hypothesised that countries with higher innovation input in the transport sector have a higher technology development level, and that the effect of innovation input on participation in technology projects is indirect. The second hypothesis is as follows:

**H2: National innovation input is positively related to the technology development level of a country.**

Early research found that factors such as the concentration of innovative activities and the size of innovative firms, which reflect the innovativeness of a sector, are essential elements of technological innovation and progress (Malerba & Orsenigo, 1996). A higher technology development level indicates that a country has accumulated a larger stock of technological knowledge and more organisations with the capabilities to develop and update technology. Such prior knowledge enables organisations to recognise, assimilate and apply new knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990), and absorptive capacity, technological opportunity and knowledge spillovers raise the innovative effort that firms dedicate to innovative activities (Nieto & Quevedo, 2005).

Participation in technology projects draws on these resources. In transport infrastructure, for example, digital transformation requires substantial research and trials (Fruth & Teuteberg, 2017), and in EU research funding, organisations with prior participation in the Framework Programmes and a stronger scientific reputation are more likely to obtain grants (Enger & Castellacci, 2016). Based on technology-push theory, a higher technology development level is therefore expected to help organisations create and take up new technologies, and to lead and join technology projects. It should be noted that the dependent variable, the level of participation, is measured at the country level as the aggregate of all stakeholder activities (total leading projects, total duration and total project cost) within that country; it does not refer to individual firm participation decisions. The hypothesis therefore predicts that countries with a higher technology development level, for example with more patents, technology exports and value added in transport equipment, tend to show higher aggregate stakeholder engagement in technology projects. The third hypothesis is as follows:

**H3: The technology development level of a country is positively related to the level of participation in transport infrastructure technology projects.**

Its abstract concerns how new-age technologies support social innovation, so it does not support the claim that "innovation applications and technology changes are found to be facilitated by technology capability". Cohen and Levinthal (1990) and Enger and Castellacci (2016) take its place.*

# F. DRAFT RESPONSE TO THE EXAMINER

> *To be placed in the Examiner 4 response letter. Page numbers will be added once the revised thesis is paginated.*

We thank the examiner for this suggestion. We agree that the term needed clarification. In the literature, technological capability usually denotes an ability to create, absorb and apply technology, whereas the three indicators of this construct (transport-related patents, medium- and high-technology exports, and value added in transport equipment manufacturing and transport services) measure the level of technology that a country has already achieved. Following the examiner's suggestion, we have renamed the construct "technology development level" (TDL) and redefined it in Section 4.3.1 as the level of technological development that a country has achieved in transport-related fields, as reflected in its technology outputs. The new definition distinguishes this concept from technological capability and is supported by the literature on measuring countries' technological achievement and capabilities with output indicators (Archibugi & Coco, 2005; Archibugi et al., 2009; Desai et al., 2002). The justification of each indicator and of hypotheses H1–H3 has been revised accordingly, and the references have been reviewed so that each one supports the statement it is cited for. The new name is used consistently in Chapters 1, 2, 4 and 7 and in the tables and figures, and a note in Section 4.3.1 explains that the published versions of this chapter used the term technology capability. To avoid confusion, the construct in the port model, technological innovation capacity, which combines innovation input and technology outputs, now has its own abbreviation, TIC.
