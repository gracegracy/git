## 1.3.1 Ports, terminals and berths

Because this thesis examines technology advancement at several levels of analysis, the units to which its findings refer must be defined precisely. Ports differ widely in size, function, governance and the activities they host, and the port literature has not settled on a single classification of ports (Bichou & Gray, 2005). This section therefore defines the port-related terms used in the thesis, drawing on official definitions and on the port and terminal literature. The definitions are summarised in Table 1, and Figure 1 shows how the units relate to one another and to the empirical studies.

A port is a node of the transport infrastructure system, in the same way as an airport or a rail freight terminal, at which waterborne transport meets land transport. In European Union law, a maritime port is defined as an area of land and water made up of the infrastructure and equipment that permits, principally, the reception of waterborne vessels, their loading and unloading, the storage of goods, the receipt and delivery of those goods, and the embarkation and disembarkation of passengers, crew and other persons (Regulation (EU) 2017/352). The port geography literature similarly describes ports as harbour areas in which marine terminal facilities transfer cargo and passengers between ships and land transport (Rodrigue & Notteboom, 2024). Two features of these definitions are important for this thesis. First, a port consists of both a land area and a water area. Second, port operations include not only the handling of cargo but also the services provided to vessels. The same regulation lists bunkering, cargo handling, mooring, passenger services, the collection of ship-generated waste and cargo residues, pilotage and towage as port services, which may be provided within the port area or on the waterway access to the port (Regulation (EU) 2017/352).

Ports can be further distinguished by their location and by the vessels and transport modes they serve. A seaport, or maritime port, receives seagoing vessels, including the ships of international liner and bulk shipping. A river port is an inland waterway port: it is located on a navigable inland waterway, such as a river, canal or lake that is not part of the sea, and it receives inland waterway vessels (Eurostat et al., 2019). A dry port has no water side. Under the Intergovernmental Agreement on Dry Ports, it is an inland location that serves as a logistics centre connected to one or more modes of transport, where goods moving in international trade are handled, stored and inspected, and customs control and formalities are carried out (ESCAP, 2013). In the dry port concept, such a terminal is linked to a seaport by high-capacity transport, typically rail, so that shippers can deliver and collect containers as if at the seaport itself (Roso et al., 2009). Because the three types of port serve different carriers, namely seagoing ships, inland waterway vessels, and trucks and trains, they also use different equipment and cargo-handling operations, and a study covering all three would need to treat them as parts of a multimodal transport network. This thesis does not take that perspective. Its empirical focus is on seaports, and Section 1.3.3 specifies which types of port enter each study.

Within a port, cargo is handled in terminals. A terminal is a part of the port's land area, with its own quay, storage areas and handling equipment, that is dedicated to a particular type of cargo or traffic, such as containers, dry bulk, liquid bulk or passengers (Rodrigue & Notteboom, 2024). In the landlord model of port management, the port authority owns the port land and basic infrastructure, plans the port and acts as regulator, while private terminal operating companies lease the terminals, provide and maintain the superstructure and equipment, and are responsible for terminal operations (World Bank, 2007). A large container port can therefore contain several container terminals run by different operators, including global terminal operators that hold terminals in many ports around the world (Notteboom & Rodrigue, 2012). A container terminal is a terminal at which containers are transferred between modes of transport, from ships to barges, trucks and trains and vice versa, using several types of handling equipment (Vis & de Koster, 2003). Along the quay of a terminal, vessels moor at berths. A berth is the location at which a vessel is stopped and secured for cargo transfer or other purposes (Bureau of Transportation Statistics, n.d.). In container terminals, the quay is either divided into a fixed number of discrete berths or treated as a continuous quay along which vessels are assigned positions (Bierwirth & Meisel, 2010).

Port, terminal and berth are therefore nested units. A port contains one or more terminals, and a terminal contains one or more berths (Figure 1). The distinction matters for measurement. The container throughput of a port is the total of the volumes handled by its terminals, whereas the equipment and operating practices of each terminal are decided by its operator (World Bank, 2007), so terminals in the same port may differ in equipment and performance. In this thesis, "port" refers to the port as a whole, "terminal" to the operational unit managed by a terminal operator, and "berth" to the mooring position at the quay. The empirical analysis in Chapter 5 is conducted at the level of container terminal operators, not of ports.

The vessels that provide services within the port are a further element of port operations. In Singapore, a harbour craft is defined in law as any vessel used in the port for any purpose (Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore Act 1996, s. 2), and harbour craft are licensed by type, including tugs, bulk liquid carriers such as bunker tankers, cargo craft and passenger craft (MPA Singapore, n.d.). Harbour craft operate in the water area of the port and provide several of the port services listed above, such as towage, bunkering and passenger transport, so they differ from the seagoing ships that call at the port. Port emission inventories accordingly treat harbour craft as a separate source category of port-related emissions, alongside ocean-going vessels, cargo-handling equipment, on-road vehicles and rail (US EPA, 2022). On this basis, harbour craft operations are treated in this thesis as part of port operations on the water side of the port, which is the setting of Chapter 6.

## 1.3.2 Container terminal layout and operational areas

Container terminals are the setting of the organisational-level study, and their internal organisation determines which equipment and technologies that study can observe. The terminal operations literature describes a container terminal as a system of linked subsystems through which containers move between the ship and the hinterland (Steenken et al., 2004; Stahlbock & Voß, 2008). At the quayside, quay cranes (QCs), also known as ship-to-shore (STS) cranes, unload and load vessels at the berth (Bierwirth & Meisel, 2010). Horizontal transport equipment, such as yard trucks, straddle carriers and automated guided vehicles (AGVs), moves containers between the quay and the storage yard (Carlo et al., 2014b). In the yard, containers are stacked in blocks and handled by yard cranes, such as rubber-tyred gantry cranes (RTGs), rail-mounted gantry cranes (RMGs) or automated stacking cranes (ASCs) (Carlo et al., 2014a). At the landside interface, containers are transferred to and from trucks at the gate and trains at rail terminals (Gharehgozli et al., 2016; Kim, 2023). Table 2 summarises these subsystems and the equipment used in each.

The arrangement of the yard blocks relative to the quay is a basic design decision. In one layout, the blocks are parallel to the quay, and containers are transferred beside the blocks, where yard trucks travel in lanes alongside them. In the other, the blocks are perpendicular to the quay, and containers are transferred at the two ends of each block, an arrangement used in automated terminals with automated stacking cranes (Kim et al., 2008; Lee & Kim, 2013; Kim, 2023). Figure 2 illustrates both layouts. The layout affects the choice of handling equipment as well as terminal productivity, and the better layout depends on design parameters such as storage requirements and the throughput capacity of the yard cranes and transporters used (Lee & Kim, 2013). For example, for terminals using automated lifting vehicles with an internal transport area of the same width, the parallel layout was found to reduce container throughput times by 4–12% compared with the perpendicular layout (Gupta et al., 2017).

Two scales of the distinction between land and water are therefore used in this thesis. At the scale of the port, the land side comprises the terminal areas, including their quays, yards and gates, and the water side comprises the port waters, including the waterway access to the port, anchorages, basins and the water alongside the berths (Regulation (EU) 2017/352; Figure 1). At the scale of the terminal, the operations literature uses "quayside" or "seaside" for the ship-to-shore interface and "landside" for the interface with trucks and trains (Steenken et al., 2004; Gharehgozli et al., 2016). In this thesis, "the land side of the port" and "the water side of the port" are used in the port-scale sense, and the terminal subsystems are referred to by their names. Figure 2 shows both scales and the parts covered by Chapters 5 and 6.

Chapter 5 examines cargo-handling equipment on the land side of the port, in the quayside and yard subsystems of container terminals, namely the number and capacity of STS cranes and yard gantry cranes. Horizontal transport equipment, gate operations and yard layout are not modelled, because the terminal-level yearbook data used in Chapter 5 do not report them consistently across operators and years (Section 5.3.2). They are treated as part of the operating context of each terminal, and their omission is discussed as a limitation in Section 5.6. Chapter 6 then turns to the water side of the port, where harbour craft operate.

## 1.3.3 Scope of the thesis and of each study

Building on these definitions, this thesis focuses on technology advancement in ports, that is, on how technologies are developed, adopted and evaluated in port infrastructure, in terminal cargo-handling equipment and in the operation of port vessels. Technology is understood to include both hardware and software. Hardware-based technologies include physical infrastructure, cargo-handling equipment, alternative-fuelled machinery and onboard energy technologies, and software-based technologies include digital and information systems, data platforms, algorithmic tools and other ICT-enabled applications. Both categories are recognised throughout the thesis, but the empirical emphasis differs between the studies, as set out below and summarised in Table 3.

Study 1 (Chapter 3) is a systematic literature review of the theories that explain port technology development. It covers studies of ports, seaports, harbours and terminals (Section 3.2) and examines the role of technology advancement in port development and the economic, institutional and organisational theories used to explain it.

Study 2 (Chapter 4) is conducted at the national level. From an investment perspective, it examines how government incentives for R&D, national innovation input and the technology development level of a country are associated with the participation of a country's organisations in technology projects. Because ports are part of the wider transport infrastructure, the chapter consists of two sub-studies. The first places ports in this wider context. It analyses transport infrastructure projects with a technological component, such as automation, digitalisation or sustainable energy, across air, rail, road, inland waterway and maritime transport, drawn from the European Commission's TRIMIS database for 35 European countries (Section 4.3.3). The second narrows the analysis to port infrastructure projects. In both sub-studies, the unit of analysis is the country, and ports enter the analysis as the location of technology projects [, which concern maritime ports and inland ports]. The chapter therefore does not examine the operations of individual ports or terminals, or the connections between ports in a multimodal transport network.
