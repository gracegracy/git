Technology advancement in ports takes place both through the improvement of established technologies and through the introduction of new ones (Section 2.1.1). In container terminals, improvements are organised around four subsystems: seaside operations at the quay, horizontal transport between the quay and the yard, storage in the yard, and landside operations at the gate and hinterland interface (Carlo et al., 2014a, 2014b; Steenken et al., 2004). Table 3 summarises the main technological improvements in each subsystem, together with improvements at the terminal-wide level. Most of them are upgrades or reconfigurations of equipment and systems that terminals already use, and they mainly raise handling capacity, productivity and reliability, or reduce energy use and emissions.

At the quay, ship-to-shore cranes have grown in outreach and lifting capacity with the size of container ships, and the adoption of new handling equipment such as post-Panamax cranes has been associated with technological progress in container terminals (Cheon et al., 2010). Spreaders have been upgraded to handle more than one container per crane cycle. Twin-lift spreaders handle two containers at a time, and tandem spreaders lift containers side by side, which raises crane productivity but requires more than one vehicle per lift and coordinated stowage planning (Chao & Lin, 2011; Kong et al., 2021). Dual-trolley quay cranes divide each crane cycle between two trolleys, so that ship-side and quay-side movements can overlap (Yue et al., 2021), and remote control allows quay cranes to be operated from a control room rather than from the crane cab (Gharehgozli et al., 2016; Knatz et al., 2022).

Horizontal transport has evolved from manned tractor–trailer units to straddle and shuttle carriers, multi-trailer systems and automated vehicles (Carlo et al., 2014b). Automated guided vehicles remove the driver, and automated lifting vehicles can also pick up and set down containers themselves, which decouples them from the cranes (Vis & Harika, 2004). In the yard, rail-mounted and automated stacking cranes allow denser stacking and lower labour input than rubber-tyred gantries or straddle carriers (Carlo et al., 2014a; Kemme, 2013). Configurations with two cranes per block, either twin cranes sharing the same rails or double cranes that can pass each other, increase the handling capacity of a block but must be coordinated to avoid interference (Gharehgozli et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2024). Positioning technologies, such as transponders, laser scanners and GPS combined with RFID, support the navigation of vehicles and the tracking of containers (Gharehgozli et al., 2016).

At the landside, gate operations have been automated with optical character recognition, often combined with cameras for damage inspection (Heilig et al., 2020). Truck appointment systems have been introduced to spread truck arrivals and reduce queuing, although an early evaluation at the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach found no evidence that the system reduced queuing or truck emissions (Giuliano & O'Brien, 2007), which shows that the effect of such systems depends on their design and use. The extended gate concept allows the seaport terminal to move containers to an inland terminal and to treat it as an extension of its own gate (Veenstra et al., 2012). Across all subsystems, terminal operating systems and related information systems plan and control the movements of equipment and containers (Heilig & Voß, 2017). Energy-related improvements to existing equipment include the electrification of cargo-handling equipment, energy storage and energy management systems (Iris & Lam, 2019). These improvements matter because a few types of equipment dominate terminal emissions. At a container terminal in the Port of Valencia, yard tractors and rubber-tyred gantries accounted for 68.1 per cent of total CO₂ emissions (Martínez-Moya et al., 2019), and electric rubber-tyred gantries have been shown to reduce energy use and CO₂ emissions substantially compared with diesel units (Yang & Chang, 2013).

These improvements show the two characteristics of technology discussed in Section 2.1.2. They are heterogeneous, ranging from mechanical upgrades of cranes to information and energy systems, and most of them are embodied in equipment, so their adoption usually takes the form of investment in new or retrofitted machines. Chapter 5 examines two of the most important items in Table 3, ship-to-shore cranes and yard gantry cranes. Table 4 complements Table 3 by summarising emerging technologies that are new to ports rather than upgrades of established terminal equipment. Where the two overlap, as with automation and electrification, Table 3 records the improvement at the level of specific terminal equipment, and Table 4 records the broader class of technology.

# PART B — DRAFT RESPONSE TO THE EXAMINER

> *To be placed in the Examiner 4 response letter. Page numbers will be added once the revised thesis is paginated.*

We thank the examiner for this suggestion. We considered merging Tables 3 and 4, but decided to keep them separate because they serve different purposes, following the distinction between improvement and innovation drawn in Section 2.1.1 (Acciaro et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2017). Table 3 now summarises improvements to established container-terminal equipment and systems, organised by terminal subsystem, whereas Table 4 summarises emerging technologies that are new to ports. We agree that the original Table 3 was too limited. It has been expanded from 7 to 15 technologies, covering seaside operations, horizontal transport, the yard, landside operations and terminal-wide systems, based on additional literature on container terminal equipment and operations (e.g., Carlo et al., 2014a, 2014b; Chao & Lin, 2011; Heilig & Voß, 2017; Iris & Lam, 2019; Kong et al., 2021; Veenstra et al., 2012). The surrounding text in Section 2.3 has been rewritten to discuss the improvements in each subsystem, to explain how Table 3 relates to Table 4, and to link the table to the cranes analysed in Chapter 5.
